How are relative A–F grades calculated?⌄

Against peers: Grade groups are sorted by their original ASCE letter, then given A, B, C, D or F using their midpoint position among graded states. A = top fifth, F = bottom fifth. States tied on the same published grade always share a relative grade, so some letters may not appear.

Against the U.S. or a state's own overall grade: C = equal to benchmark; B/A = one/two or more ASCE steps above; D/F = one/two or more steps below.

Important: These relative letters are illustrative, not official ASCE results. Report years and category coverage differ. No grade or insufficient peer coverage means no relative grade.

How the relative A–F grades work

Peer A–F: Group states by their published ASCE grades. The midpoint percentile of each tied grade group determines its relative letter: top 20% A, next 20% B, middle 20% C, next 20% D, bottom 20% F. Tied states stay together, so a letter can be unused. Sectors require at least 10 graded peer states.

Versus 2025 U.S. A–F: Equal to the national grade is C, one ASCE grade step above is B, two or more above is A, one step below is D, and two or more below is F. This is not an official ASCE grade.

Versus state overall A–F: Uses the same five-category rule with the state’s overall grade as baseline. It measures category strength relative to its own overall assessment, not a direct interstate comparison.

Limitations: Relative grades are exploratory labels based on ordinal letter grades from different report years. A peer letter does not estimate actual infrastructure quality or percentile from underlying engineering measurements.

Coverage, comparability & provenance

  • Every graded state has an overall grade and a report year; states without one remain unranked.
  • Sector data marked “complete” were taken from a full ASCE grade breakdown. “Partial” means only some values were independently verified.
  • Different report years, category selections, underlying conditions, and source vintages limit cross-state comparisons.
  • ASCE itself explains that state reports do not always cover the national report's full 18-category scope.
